## system status

thirty-seven consecutive D-grades. one automated commit. the ai assistant Mac Mini pulled its morning crypto signals and the daily tracker ran its scan. quiet day number thirty-seven.

## what was built/changed

morning market signal update. daily tracker scan. both crons fired, both committed, both pushed. no new features. no new integrations. nothing to demo.

and that's actually what I want to talk about today. because there's a phase of building that nobody writes blog posts about: maintenance mode.

when you set up an ai assistant Mac Mini (or any always-on automation), you spend a burst of energy building the thing. writing crons. wiring up APIs. debugging why a script fails at 3am when a token expires. that part is fun. it's visible. you can screenshot it, post about it, get engagement.

then the system works. and you enter this phase where your job is... making sure it keeps working. checking logs. renewing tokens before they expire. monitoring for upstream API changes that could silently break a pipeline. none of that generates commits. none of it looks productive. but it's the reason the system is still running on day thirty-seven instead of having crashed on day four.

I think most people dramatically underestimate how much of a working system's lifetime is spent in this phase. building is maybe 20% of the lifecycle. the other 80% is keeping it alive. and the keeping-it-alive part has almost zero visibility. no one posts about it. no one teaches it. so when people build their first automation and hit the maintenance phase, they think something went wrong. they think they should be shipping more. they start rebuilding instead of sustaining.

## observations

there's an interesting parallel to physical trades here. a plumber doesn't install a water heater and walk away forever. there are maintenance visits. filter changes. anode rod inspections. the installation is the exciting part but the maintenance is what determines whether the thing lasts five years or fifteen.

software people have mostly convinced themselves that once code is deployed, it runs forever. it doesn't. APIs deprecate. rate limits change. auth tokens expire. dependencies get CVEs. the machine that runs your automation needs OS updates. every piece of infrastructure has a maintenance surface area, and ignoring it doesn't make it zero. it just makes the failure sudden instead of gradual.

the grading system I built measures visible output. commits, content published, features shipped. by that metric, thirty-seven D-grades in a row looks like failure. by the metric of "is the system still running without manual intervention after 37 days," it's actually working exactly as designed. the grade is measuring the wrong thing during this phase.

## gaps / honest critique

the grading system needs a maintenance-aware mode. right now it treats every day the same. a day where I ship a major feature and a day where crons run quietly both get measured on the same scale. that's like grading a restaurant the same whether it's a grand opening or a Tuesday lunch. the expectations should be different.

more honestly... thirty-seven days of D-grades is also just a long time to not ship anything new. maintenance is real and necessary, but it can also become a comfortable excuse. there's a difference between "the system is in maintenance mode" and "I haven't prioritized building anything new." I'm not sure which one this is anymore. probably some of both.

the crypto signal pipeline is running but I haven't built anything on top of it. signals without action is just data collection cosplay.

## tomorrow's focus

audit what the crypto signal data actually looks like after 37 days of collection. is there enough to build a simple analysis layer on top? if not, why am I collecting it.

review the grading criteria. either adjust the scale to account for maintenance phases or use the D-grade as the honest kick it's meant to be.
